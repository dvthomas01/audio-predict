Attention vs. Recurrence: Benchmarking Transformers and LSTMs for Music Spectral Forecasting

Introduction

With the advent of multi-model models arriving at scale, a unique application: generative music has taken the world by storm. Whether it be music startups seeking to create indistinguishable recreations of popular artists' voices, or simply Professor Shefield experimenting with catchy songs for his 18.600 lectures, the demand for audibly coherent generation is higher than ever. In the past, the modeling of time-series data, an inherent format of music, has required capturing both immediate textural details and long-term structural dependencies. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, initially found great success in this arena due to their ability to retain information over time [1].

However, due to the rise of Self-Attention mechanisms, Transformers have gained ground in sequence modeling tasks, raising the research question of their efficacy in audio prediction [2]. This constitutes a contentious topic in deep learning research, as opposed to Natural Language Processing (NLP); audio data’s complexity lies within its texture, as we are not only evaluating mathematically consistent predictions but audibly coherent predictions as well.

Related Works

Even with the advent of Transformer architectures and their NLP success, their dominance of time-series forecasting data is still up for debate. Historically, LSTMs have been the standard for computer-driven music composition, with foundational work by Eck and Schmidhuber going back to 2002, where they trained LSTMs to learn blues improvisation music [3]. However, recent benchmarking studies in other applications yield differing results. For example, Hittawe et al. concluded that LSTMs can outperform Transformers in financial stock data forecasting, showcasing that even with the advent of Transformers, they still struggle with continuous numerical data [4]. On the other hand, Weissenborn et al. concluded that Transformers are significantly stronger with complex and long-range trends in autoregressive video modeling than LSTMs [5].

Recent work in the audio domain regarding Transformers has showcased specialized architectures. Lu et al. introduced SpecTNT, a custom Transformer architecture for music classification, as they brought forth the failures of standard Transformers in capturing spectral dependencies due to the standard vectorized representation of frequency [8]. Their work proposed a complex “Transformer in a Transformer” solution that could model the texture of complex audio data.

Additionally, a major gap in the current literature is the "format" of the music being predicted. Much of the successful work involving Transformers in music, such as the Music Transformer, relies on symbolic data (MIDI events) rather than raw audio [6].

Methods

Data

We chose to utilize the Medley-Solos-DB [9] which contains a sizeable dataset of 7 different classes of instruments. The data was pre-processed into spectral representations using 128 frequency bins. The raw audio, originally sampled at a high sampling rate, is converted into Mel Spectrograms. This transformation reduces the dimensionality of the data significantly.

We justified selecting Mel Spectrograms over Short-Time Fourier Transforms (STFT) for two reasons. Firstly, the Mel scale is engineered to mimic human perception of audio. Secondly, to verify the perceptual quality of our model outputs, we utilized the BigVGAN vocoder to invert the generated spectrograms back into audible waveforms for qualitative listening tests.

Architectures

To rigorously compare sequential modeling capabilities, we implemented two distinct architectures: a standard Autoregressive Transformer and a Baseline LSTM.

1. Autoregressive Transformer

We utilized a causal Transformer architecture with sinusoidal positional encodings. To ensure the model could capture long-range dependencies, we trained three size variants. The "Large" variant utilized 8 layers, 16 attention heads, and an embedding dimension of 512.

2. LSTM Baseline

We implemented a standard LSTM network. The model projects the 128-dimensional input into a hidden space (256 units), processes temporal steps via a Long Short-Term Memory cell, and projects back to the spectral dimension.

Due to the differing convergence properties of the architectures, we optimized the training hyperparameters for each model type separately:

  • Transformer: Trained for 35 Epochs with a Batch Size of 32 and a Learning Rate of 1e-4 (AdamW).
  • LSTM: Trained for 20 Epochs with a Batch Size of 64 and a Learning Rate of 1e-3 (Adam).

Experimental Design

We decided to focus on three primary experiments to evaluate the generative capabilities and the subsequent robustness of the models.

1. Future Horizon Forecasting (Varying K)

In this experiment, we test the models ability to sustain audinly-coherent generation over long periods. We provide a fixed context window of the first 150 frames (out of 253 total) and ask the model to predict the next K frames autoregressively.

  • Context: 150 frames
  • Prediction Horizon (K): [20, 60, 100] frames

2. Context Sensitivity (Varying Input)

Next, we evaluate how much historical information each architecture requires to make accurate predictions. We fix the prediction horizon to a constant 60 frames and vary the size of the input context provided to the model. This test is conducted using the Medium size variant of both models to provide a standard testing ground.

  • Context Sizes: [50, 100, 150] frames
  • Prediction Horizon: 60 frames (constant)

3. Scalability Analysis

Finally, we seek to compare the performance of the Small, Medium, and Large variants (defined in the Architectures section) on a standardized task: 150 context frames predicting 60 future frames. This establishes whether the Transformer scales more effectively with increased parameter counts compared to the LSTM.

Robustness Testing

To assess generalization, we introduce perturbations to the test set data. We evaluate the models on audio that has undergone:

  • Pitch Shifting: Transposing the audio up or down by one octave.
  • Time Stretching: Increasing or decreasing the playback speed.

These tests determine if the models have learned fundamental musical structures or simply memorized specific spectral patterns in the training set.

Evaluation Metrics

Since the output is a continuous spectral representation, we treat the generation as a regression problem.

  • Mean Squared Error (MSE): We calculate the pixel-wise MSE between the generated Mel Spectrogram and the ground truth spectrogram of the original audio. This provides a direct quantitative measure of reconstruction accuracy.
  • Visual Inspection: We visually compare the generated spectrogram heatmaps against the ground truth to assess the definition of harmonics and the clarity of transients.

Quantitative Analysis

To rigorously evaluate the Autoregressive Transformer’s capabilities in spectral forecasting, we conducted three distinct benchmarks focusing on forecasting horizons, context sensitivity, and model scalability. All metrics reported below were computed on the held-out test set (N=5,841) using autoregressive generation to reflect real-world inference conditions.

Model Scalability and The "Capacity Sweet Spot"

Contrary to the standard deep learning assumption that "larger is better," our experiments revealed a non-linear relationship between parameter count and model performance. We evaluated Small (~544k), Medium (~4.1M), and Large (~26.6M) variants on a standardized task (Context=150, Horizon K=60).

Model Variant Parameters MSE ↓ Frame Acc (10%) ↑ R² ↑
Small ~0.5M 2.55 26.53% 0.47
Medium ~4.1M 2.32 28.25% 0.52
Large ~26.6M 2.89 25.73% 0.40
Table 1: Performance comparison across model sizes for a fixed forecast horizon of K=60. The Medium model achieves the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) and highest Frame Accuracy, indicating a capacity "sweet spot."

The Medium model consistently outperformed both the Small and Large variants. Surprisingly, the Large model performed worse than the Small baseline across several metrics, exhibiting an 11% higher MSE (2.89 vs 2.32) and a 23% lower R² than the Medium model. This suggests that for the available dataset size (~12k training samples), the Large model suffered from overfitting or optimization difficulties inherent to its complex loss landscape.

Forecast Horizon (Varying K)

We evaluated the Medium model's ability to maintain coherence over extended timeframes by varying the prediction horizon K ∈ [20, 60, 100].

[INSERT FIGURE 1: Line Graph showing MSE/Correlation vs. Forecast Horizon (K)]
Figure 1: Degradation of prediction quality as the forecast horizon (K) increases. While MSE increases due to error accumulation, Correlation remains relatively stable, indicating the model preserves structural patterns even as pixel-perfect accuracy drifts.
  • Short-term (K=20): The model demonstrated strong fidelity (MSE=1.41, Corr=0.85), effectively continuing the immediate spectral textures.
  • Long-term (K=100): We observed expected performance degradation (MSE=2.83, Corr=0.71). Frame accuracy dropped from 36.4% to 24.6%.

This degradation is inherent to autoregressive generation. Since the model relies on its own previous outputs to generate the next frame, minor errors at step t propagate and compound by step t+n. However, the correlation remaining above 0.7 at 100 frames (approx. 2.3 seconds) indicates the model retains structural coherence even as precision drifts.

Context Sensitivity

We analyzed the impact of historical information by varying the input context window [50, 100, 150].

[INSERT FIGURE 2: Bar Chart or Line Graph of MSE vs. Context Size]
Figure 2: Impact of context window size on validation MSE. A context of 150 frames yields the optimal performance, though diminishing returns are observed relative to the computational cost.
  • Context=150 yielded the best performance (MSE=2.22), confirming that longer history aids in capturing temporal dependencies.
  • Context=50 resulted in higher error (MSE=2.31) and "smoother" predictions, as the model lacked sufficient history to infer complex harmonic trajectories.

The improvement from 50 to 150 frames suggests that while context is critical, there are diminishing returns; the most relevant musical information is likely contained within the immediate 3-second window.

Visual inspection of the generated Mel spectrograms supports the quantitative metrics and highlights specific architectural behaviors.

Frequency Definition

As seen in Figure 3, the Transformer architecture excels at reconstructing low-frequency content (0–30 bins). The fundamental frequencies and bass lines are rendered with high energy and sharp definition, closely matching the Ground Truth. However, a "spectral smoothing" effect is observable in the high frequencies (>70 bins). While the Ground Truth exhibits sharp, transient details in the upper registers, the Transformer’s predictions often blur these textures. This is a common phenomenon in MSE-optimized regression models, which tend to average out uncertainty.

Temporal Coherence

The Transformer successfully maintains the rhythmic structure of the audio. Unlike non-autoregressive baselines which might produce disjointed blocks, the Transformer's diagonal harmonic lines remain continuous. However, as the prediction horizon extends to the right side of the spectrograms, a gradual loss of harmonic definition is observable, visually corroborating the error accumulation noted in Section 1.2.
